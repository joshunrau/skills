# Email

Use for drafted emails and short messages to other people. Apply the shared guidance in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md) alongside this reference.

## Sentence Devices

For G8, the email stance-adverb inventory is: Unfortunately, Honestly, Also, Otherwise, Overall, So. See E7 for bad news and E19 for familiar-recipient updates.

For G10, use first person for an assessment (", which I think works much better for the timeline"). For G11, mirror a correspondent's pleasantry with "... as well" before turning to business.

## Openings and Closings

**E1.** An initiating email opens with a purpose statement: "I wanted to follow up regarding ..."; "I just wanted to let you know that ...". A reply opens with a short acknowledgment before the content: "Thanks for the quick reply." Replies to factual or yes/no questions skip the acknowledgment and go straight to the answer (see G17).

**E2.** Default salutation is "Hi [first name]," for everyone, including senior recipients and administrators. A title is used only for a prospective contact you have not yet met.

**E3.** Always sign with the short informal form of your first name, even when correspondents use the full form. Default sign-off is "Thanks,". Use "All the best," or "Best regards," on declines, pleas, and farewells; "Thank you," on requests to clients or external contacts.

**E4.** When the message hands the recipient something to check or act on, the last line before the sign-off is a "Please let me know ..." prompt. When the message is itself the request, the prompt is absent.

**E5.** After describing a step taken on your own initiative, ask for ratification: "Please let me know if this is okay".

## Requests and Decisions

**E6.** When you need a decision, frame the question as two named alternatives: "Should I send the summary to the client directly, or would you prefer to review it first?"; "Should we use the existing template, or should I draft a new one?"

**E7.** Sentence-initial "Unfortunately," introduces bad news or a constraint in the first or second sentence of the message, followed by a plain statement with no further softening beyond the adverb.

## Apologies and Refusals

**E9.** An apology is one clause with an intensifier, immediately followed by the cause, with no further elaboration: "I’m very sorry for the late reply. I misjudged how long the review would take."

**E10.** A refusal or negative outcome is one declarative sentence with "unfortunately" and/or "for now," then a goodwill or forward-looking line. No extended justification.

**E11.** When ending a collaboration or asking a concession from someone who has invested substantial effort, close with thanks aimed at that specific effort: "Thanks again for sticking with this and for the extra hours last week." Not a generic "thanks."

## Tone and Register

**E12.** Email prose uses "just" as a softener ("I just wanted to check"; "you can just delete"), "really" for emphasis, "for now" as a provisional marker, and plain intensifiers ("a fair bit," "huge," "totally"). These never appear in formal prose. The register tightens for unfamiliar or external recipients; these markers drop out.

**E13.** "really," "totally," and vivid descriptors cluster in emails that decline, apologize, or vent, and are absent from neutral informational emails.

**E14.** Colloquial idioms ("dropped the ball," "hit a wall," "in over their heads") appear only when narrating or negotiating dealings with people, in email to insiders. They never appear in formal argument sections.

**E15.** "okay" is always spelled out (never "OK," "ok," "fine," or "alright") when seeking or giving approval: "Please let me know if this is okay."

**E16.** Acknowledgments and quick evaluations are written as subjectless fragments, especially at the open or close of a message: "Very solid work."; "Much appreciated."; "No worries at all."

**E17.** "!" appears only on greetings, thanks, and congratulations ("Congrats on the launch!"; "Much appreciated!"), never on content.

**E18.** When proposing a meeting, give open availability with a single named exception rather than listing slots: "Any time next week, with the exception of Thursday."

**E19.** With recipients you know well, an update narrative may open with "So," or "So to update you,". Limited to familiar recipients; also serves as a mid-paragraph narrative transition.
